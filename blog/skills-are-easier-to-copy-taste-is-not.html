In the Age of AI, Skills Are Easier to Copy. Taste Is Not.

August 28, 2026 · 7 min read

We are living in a time when almost everything is becoming easier to learn, create, and reproduce.

A few years ago, becoming a good designer, writer, programmer, or researcher required years of practice. You had to develop technical skills, understand the tools, and spend countless hours learning how things worked.

Today, AI has changed much of that.

You can generate a beautiful design in seconds, write an article in minutes, build a website without knowing much about programming, or produce something that once required an entire creative team.

And this is only the beginning.

As AI continues to improve, I believe technical skills will become increasingly accessible.

But there's something I've been thinking about lately.

When everyone has access to the same tools, what makes one person's work different from another's?

For me, the answer is increasingly clear.

Taste.

The Difference Between Skill and Taste

I think skill and taste are two very different things.

Skill is knowing how to do something.

Taste is knowing what is worth doing, what looks right, what feels appropriate, and perhaps most importantly, what should be left out.

Imagine giving two people the same camera, the same lighting equipment, and the same editing software.

Both understand photography.

Both know how to operate the equipment.

Yet one produces an ordinary photograph, while the other creates something that stays in your mind.

The difference isn't necessarily technical ability.

It's how they see things.

What they notice.

What they choose to include.

And what they deliberately leave outside the frame.

I believe the same principle applies to almost everything, from business and technology to architecture, fashion, and the way we live.

Money Can Buy Luxury, but Not Necessarily Taste

I've always found this particularly interesting in classic menswear.

You can walk into an expensive boutique and buy a suit from one of the finest fashion houses in the world.

You can spend thousands of dollars on Italian fabrics, handmade shoes, and luxury accessories.

But none of those things automatically makes you well-dressed.

A man wearing an expensive suit can still look less elegant than someone wearing a much more affordable one.

Because dressing well involves more than the price of your clothes.

It's about understanding proportions, fabrics, colors, silhouettes, and the relationship between clothing and the person wearing it.

A beautifully tailored double-breasted suit might look wonderful in one setting but feel completely inappropriate in another.

A relaxed linen jacket might be a better choice for an afternoon at a café than a structured business suit.

Knowing the difference is part of developing taste.

And taste isn't something you receive automatically when you purchase an expensive product.

Money gives you access to more choices. Taste helps you make better ones.

Taste Is Built Through Experience

I don't believe people are simply born with good taste.

Of course, some people may have a natural sensitivity to beauty, design, or proportion.

But I think taste is largely developed through experience.

The books we read.

The places we visit.

The architecture we admire.

The films we watch.

The people we meet.

The clothes we wear.

Even the restaurants we choose and the way we arrange our living spaces.

All these experiences gradually shape our understanding of what we appreciate.

When someone spends years studying classic menswear, they begin to notice details that other people might overlook.

The way a lapel rolls.

The balance of a jacket.

The texture of a particular fabric.

The difference between something that looks expensive and something that looks genuinely elegant.

Someone interested in architecture might develop the same sensitivity toward buildings, furniture, lighting, and space.

Over time, these observations become part of how we see the world.

And I think that's what makes taste so personal.

Two people can visit the same city, read the same book, or admire the same painting, yet take away completely different things.

Because they bring different experiences, perspectives, and values.

AI Can Reproduce an Aesthetic. But Can It Replace Your Judgment?

Today, I can ask AI to create something in the style of a luxury fashion campaign.

I can generate a beautiful interior, an elegant photograph, or a sophisticated brand identity.

And the results can be impressive.

But here's the interesting part.

AI can generate hundreds of possibilities.

Someone still has to decide which one is right.

Which design feels too complicated?

Which photograph has the right atmosphere?

Which color combination feels timeless rather than trendy?

Which detail should be removed?

Sometimes, the most important creative decision isn't what to add.

It's knowing when to stop.

AI can help us explore possibilities, compare alternatives, and even improve our judgment.

But having access to a thousand beautiful options doesn't automatically mean we know which one to choose.

That's why I believe taste will become even more important as creative tools become more powerful.

When producing something becomes easier, deciding what deserves to be produced becomes more valuable.

The Same Principle Applies to Business

I've spent a significant part of my time working with business systems, technology, and digital transformation.

And I've noticed something similar.

Two companies can use the same software.

The same AI tools.

The same automation platforms.

Even similar business processes.

But their results can be completely different.

One company builds a complicated system with dozens of unnecessary features.

Another builds something simple, practical, and genuinely useful.

Both may have access to the same technology.

But they don't necessarily have the same judgment.

Knowing how to build a system is one thing.

Knowing what kind of system should exist in the first place is another.

Sometimes, good judgment means deciding not to automate something.

Not to introduce another tool.

Not to add another feature.

Not to make something more complicated simply because the technology allows it.

I think this is where technical ability and taste begin to overlap.

Skill helps us execute.

Taste helps us recognize quality.

And judgment helps us decide what actually matters.

The Art of Choosing

As I've grown older, I've become increasingly interested in the idea of intentional living.

Not necessarily owning more things.

Not constantly chasing whatever is new.

But becoming more thoughtful about what I choose to bring into my life.

A wardrobe doesn't become better simply because it contains more clothes.

A home doesn't become more beautiful because it contains more expensive furniture.

And a business doesn't become more successful simply because it uses more technology.

Sometimes, improvement comes from removing things.

Choosing fewer pieces.

Creating more space.

Simplifying a process.

Understanding what matters and what doesn't.

I think taste is closely connected to this ability to choose.

Not everything beautiful needs to be owned.

Not every opportunity needs to be pursued.

Not every trend deserves our attention.

And not everything we are capable of creating needs to exist.

Good taste isn't only about knowing what to choose. It's also about knowing what to refuse.

What Will Matter in the Age of AI?

I don't believe skills will become irrelevant.

We will still need people who understand their craft, develop expertise, and know how to execute ideas properly.

And taste alone, without the ability to turn ideas into reality, has its limitations.

But I do believe the relationship between skill and taste is changing.

In a world where technical execution becomes increasingly accessible, the ability to recognize quality, make thoughtful decisions, and develop an independent perspective may become an even greater advantage.

We shouldn't stop learning technical skills.

But perhaps we should also spend more time developing our judgment.

Read beyond our professions.

Study art, design, history, and culture.

Travel when we can.

Pay attention to craftsmanship.

Learn to appreciate things beyond their price tags.

And occasionally question why we like something in the first place.

Because the goal shouldn't simply be to produce more.

It should be to create, choose, and live better.

AI may eventually help almost anyone produce something technically impressive.

But that doesn't mean every result will be meaningful, beautiful, or worth keeping.

And perhaps that's the distinction we should pay more attention to.

Skill teaches us how to create.

Taste helps us understand what is worth creating.

And in a world where almost anything can be generated, knowing what truly matters might become one of the most valuable abilities we can develop.

— Bhone Nyan Soe
